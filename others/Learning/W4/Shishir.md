Learning the Quantum Fourier Transformation

Joseph Fourier figures out while studying how heat flows, that any function can be decomposed into a sum of sines and cosines. This kind of decomposition is called the Fourier Transformation. The quantum equivalent is the quantum Fourier Transformation. It is important that I understand this because it is one of the fundamental and common subroutine in the implementation of some of the common quantum algorithms such as phase estimation, amplitude estimation and Shor's factoring algorithm. 

First I try to understand the classical Fourier Transform also known as the Discrete Fourier Transform. There are two variants of the classical Fourier Transform, the Continuous one and the Discrete one. First I learned the continuous one. The idea is if I have a signal in the time domain where its amplitude which is a function of time, I can use the Continuous Fourier Transform to convert the signal and break it down to its individual component sines and cosine waves. I convert it to the frequency domain and get to see individual waves that have combined and formed the original signal at hand. The CFT is represented by an integral where the original signal is multiplied with a complex exponential which represents both the sines and cosines and includes the frequency. If I want to know a certain frequency is present in our original signal, I simply input that frequency and if its there then there will be constructive interference and we will get a larger value compared to if the frequency is not there.
